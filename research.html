Research

I am interested in improving migration policy, seeing the potential it delivers for migrants, their home communities, and the destination communities. My research has so far focused on the migration industry, broadly defined as the aggregate of productive enterprises engaged in migration-related services for profit. This includes the full spectrum from language and vocational training schools, recruitment agencies, to human traffickers. They make migration possible, but raises a host of issues of labor abuse, auditing small firms, and market power. This makes my subfields within economics something like labor, development, IO, political economy, and/or behavioral. An overview of my work in this arena is compiled in a slide deck here, recently presented at the Harvard Predoc Workshop.

I am extremely policy-focused in this domain and am interested in incorporating a variety of methods to this end, from qualitative work to structural modelling. My practical experiences in this field include the UNHCR, where I conducted legal research and fielded calls from asylum seekers and detainees, and Japan International Cooperation Agency (JICA), where I initiated many of my ongoing research projects around the recruitment of migrant workers in Greater Asia.

Other "thoughts in progress" include: experimentation and learning among policymakers (and how research can complement and improve them), intersubjectivity and the formation of worldviews (from my experiences in a liberal arts college).

Working Papers

  • Migration Industries and the Making of an International Labor Market
    Please email me for a draft-in-progress.

    Abstract: As international labor migration increased in greater Asia from 1992 to 2009, migrant supply chains characterized by smaller, more direct hiring were increasingly replaced by ones that involve large employers and intermediaries. A simple model shows that such industrialization of migration can increase both its scale and volatility. This is because intermediaries that match origin-country workers to destination-country employers exploit economies of scale by aggregating demand for marginal units with lower willingness to pay. Consistent with this model, I provide evidence for i) large increases in both workers and employers from the first entry of intermediaries into the labor market and ii) higher wage elasticities of demand for intermediaries and large firms compared to smaller employers who hire directly. More opportunities abroad, therefore, came with increased uncertainty, which has policy implications for migrant protection, brain gain, and risk-sharing.

  • Fringes of the Migration Industry
    Originally my undergraduate thesis. Thesis Version; slides from PIER Conference (2024)

    Abstract: Fraud and labor abuse among recruiters of international migrant workers have persisted despite improvements in information provision and enforcement. I offer a more structural explanation: intermediaries set higher prices on the inelastic side of the market, and competition for employer demand in the downstream market drives recruiters to raise the migrant-side "price" above legal limits (fraud). Using administrative data from the Philippines, I first document that fraud is indeed concentrated among fringe recruiters, those with small market share, and that fraudsters tend to be newer entrants and accept lower wages for their workers from employers. I then show that more competition due to negative employer demand shocks increases the likelihood of recruiters i) exiting the market or ii) becoming fraudulent, both of which are more pronounced for fringe recruiters. The total prevalence of fraud, therefore, is determined by the net of the two effects, suggesting roles for entry and exit regulation in the industry.

Work in Progress

  • Migrants' Jobs

    Abstract: I collect daily observations from centralized postings of overseas jobs in six of the largest migrant-sending countries across greater Asia (India, Indonesia, Nepal, Pakistan, Philippines, Sri Lanka). The harmonized job-level dataset includes, at minimum, the name of the destination-country employer, origin-country intermediary, occupation, and number of vacancies; and often, their wages, specific qualifications, and non-pecuniary amenities. The high-frequency data collection allows observation of both demand (job orders from the same set of destination countries to many origin countries) and equilibrium supply (sorting of similar workers to different destinations based on job characteristics), which I validate using aggregate-level administrative statistics. This allows me to answer several pressing policy questions. i) Migrant protection policies often result in trading off quality of migration with quantity, since employers can substitute across origin countries. How much is and what are the determinants of this elasticity of substitution? ii) Anecdotally, East Asian countries with severe labor shortages are increasingly struggling to recruit migrant workers despite the (diminishing but still high) wage premium. Is this empirically founded, and if so why? iii) Migrants faced with supporting family often get trapped in circular migration to more repressive, lower benefit destinations (e.g., GCC). Are migrants making optimal destination choices, and if not, how can policy shift their choices?
